## Rest (07.05.26)

![](/images/about/background/4f05748d74bbb245c4febed3bdcb7747.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/2aa3e1c6e44a9c41f47562ca2fbfc818.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/db737e5fcdfefef35f21e57c64867c4e.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/cd9c959f7d133f34bca5c46f9650825b.jpg)

My doctor recently gave me the green light to step down my antidepressant medication, paired with a clear directive. I needed to actively practice genuine rest and mindfulness if I wanted to keep anxiety at bay as I transitioned out. In the past, I completely misunderstood such instructions. I thought rest meant stillness, so I tried to sit on the couch and do nothing, waiting for peace to arrive. My mind did what anxious minds do. It sprinted toward an uncertain future, and fear filled the space I had left empty.

That experience helped me see something I had long resisted. Anxiety often behaves like a bodyguard that mistakes vigilance for safety. It keeps watch, scans for threats, and never sleeps, but in doing so, it exhausts the very person it is trying to protect.

Neuroscience gives language to this inner turmoil. When the brain is left idle, the Default Mode Network becomes especially active, and instead of producing peace, that inward turn can intensify rumination, self-referential thinking, and worry. The resting brain is hard at work, and its activity is closely tied to self-reflection, mind-wandering, memory, and imagining the future. Groundbreaking neuroscientist Marcus Raichle, who pioneered functional brain imaging at Washington University, helped show that the resting brain is not inactive at all, but busy in a deeply internal way. Forced idleness can feel less like recovery and more like being trapped alone with one’s own noise.

We tend to think of rest as an absence, a clearing away of activity until nothing remains. What if true rest is actually a presence?

This is why active rest has been a revelation to me. Sometimes rest is not achieved by doing nothing, but by doing something that is quietly absorbing, familiar, and life-giving. In flow states, attention narrows, the self-conscious parts of the mind soften, and the brain works with unusual efficiency. Exercise neuroscientist Arne Dietrich described this as transient hypofrontality, a temporary quieting of the prefrontal cortex during deep engagement. The mind can rest while the body is in motion, especially when that motion is rooted in competence, attention, and delight.

The experience of deep focus reminds me of the physical laws of rotational motion. Pushing a heavy flywheel from a dead stop demands a massive input of torque to overcome its moment of inertia, its inherent resistance to moving at all. Once the wheel spins, it builds kinetic energy, and its momentum fiercely resists sudden changes in direction. A deeply engaged brain acts like this spinning mass, relying on dense neural networks to stay in steady motion.

Overcoming initial resistance takes intense effort, and the brain must still burn energy continuously to fight off the friction of distractions, actively suppressing passing alarms to maintain a dynamically stable equilibrium. Because the mental system possesses heavy momentum, external interruptions struggle to derail it.

Here is where the modern productivity narrative twists something beautiful into something harmful. We hear that the ideal life is one where our work overlaps perfectly with our gifts, our passions, and our natural flow states. If we can just find the right career, burnout will disappear, and rest will become unnecessary. That promise is seductive, yet it is profoundly incomplete.

We were never meant to make our jobs into our salvation. We cannot ask our work to carry the weight of our emotional healing, our identity, and our worth. In Seated with Christ, one of my favorite books, Penn State professor and speaker Heather Holleman points to a different foundation altogether. Scripture says believers are seated with Christ, treasured by God and given a place at His table. That matters because if our peace depends on constant competence, we become spiritually and emotionally fragile. Any task that exposes weakness starts to feel like a threat instead of a normal human reality.

True wholeness requires more than ease. Renowned research professor Brené Brown, whose decades of study at the University of Houston transformed how we understand human emotion, defines vulnerability as uncertainty, risk, and emotional exposure, which means growth always includes moments that cannot be optimized away. If we only seek activities that make us feel brilliant and in control, we may soothe ourselves, but we will not become more whole. Life includes tasks we are bad at, relationships we cannot manage, and seasons when we must stay present without feeling in command.

Connection matters deeply here. Psychiatrist Curt Thompson, whose clinical work bridges neurobiology and Christian spiritual formation, emphasizes that healing is not merely private. It happens in a safe relationship, where shame can be faced, and truth can be told. Journalist Sebastian Junger makes a similar point in another of my favorite books, Tribe, noting that humans do not mind hardship, but rather thrive on it, while what they truly mind is not feeling necessary. Drawing on his years reporting from war zones and isolated frontline communities, Junger shows that we do not thrive merely by becoming efficient. We thrive when we are needed, known, and woven into an ordinary community.

This answers a modern misunderstanding about peace. Some voices, like a post I scrolled past on Instagram while procrastinating on writing this essay, suggest that true peace means becoming so self-contained that you no longer care whether you are known, understood, or seen. That is not peace—that is numbness dressed up as freedom . Human beings were made for relationships, and the longing to be known is a basic part of being human. Real peace does not erase the desire to be seen, but instead meets it safely.

To me, true peace is the deep confidence that I am already known, understood, and seen, rather than an indifference to being known. I do not have to perform to be visible, and I do not have to harden myself to be safe. I can relax because belonging is not something I must invent.

This is where Heather Holleman and Sebastian Junger ultimately meet. Holleman reminds us that our belonging is first rooted in Christ, where we are seated, held, and given a place before we achieve anything. Junger reminds us that this same need for belonging is woven into our social nature, showing that we are healthier when we live in communities where we matter and are needed. One points to spiritual identity, the other to human design, yet both insist on the same truth. We are not meant to live as isolated selves. We are made for connection, and our peace deepens when our lives are received rather than merely performed.

That also reframes the value of mundane work. Early twentieth-century teacher Oswald Chambers wrote powerfully about drudgery as a proving ground for character, and the life of Christ itself sanctified ordinary service most unexpectedly, as the Lord of glory knelt to wash dirty feet. That image matters because it reminds us that hidden, repetitive, unglamorous acts are not beneath us. They are often where love becomes concrete.

Active rest should not become another performance metric. Neither should it be a clever way to make ourselves more productive, more marketable, or more efficient. It ought to be a quiet return to life. It is playing the piano with no audience, taking a walk without turning it into a fitness goal, helping someone with a small practical need, making pupusas, folding laundry, watering plants, or doing one ordinary thing with full attention and no need for applause, only appreciation.

That kind of rest steadies the mind because it relocates us. It reminds us that we are not machines trying to maximize output. We are embodied, relational, spiritual people who need movement, beauty, service, and stillness in their proper measure. In my early adulthood, I am glad to have learned that restoration often comes not from retreating into inertness, but from entering simple, uncalculated motion with a settled heart.

From that place of belonging, even the most ordinary acts can become restful, because they are no longer attempts to earn worth. They are simply ways of living from a peace that has already been given.

## The Art of Uncalculating (06.06.26)

Since graduating, I have noticed a frantic shift in how my peers talk about the future. For the first time, we are navigating a life undefined by the rigid structures of academia, and doing so in a modern landscape reshaped by advanced technologies, where the pressure to instantly optimize our lives is breeding a distinct kind of panic.

Recently, I saw a LinkedIn post online from someone my age that shared a thought that caught my attention. They argued that because modern-day technologies like AI have democratized everything, our twenties are a rapidly closing window. In their view, we have a short and frantic timeline to achieve massive success before the dust settles and the world locks us out. It is the ultimate modern trap: believing that brilliant moments, solo-founder titles, and public triumphs are the only metrics that matter.

While I understood the urgency, I couldn't help but cringe at the post. This frantic urgency assumes that life is a desperate race toward an immediate goal. It completely ignores the truth that real development takes immense patience, and trying to force ourselves into a perfect shape overnight is a deeply flawed approach.

Of course, it is normal to want to adapt to a changing world. But this constant anxiety makes us feel like we are always failing. It creates an "I am behind" mindset. We become so obsessed with reaching thrilling peaks of success that we begin to despise ordinary routines or the quiet "in-between" periods of life. Yet, it is exactly in the quiet, repetitive tasks of daily life, not the moments of public applause, where our true character is formed and tested.

I know this exhausting cycle intimately. Especially in mid-college, my entire identity was tied to my productivity and my need to please others. After facing severe mental exhaustion and disorders, I underwent a deep internal reevaluation. I stopped judging my life by how much I could output or by temporary professional titles that quickly fade away. Titles and status fluctuate. Instead, I learned that true growth requires slowing down to carefully nurture your life, ensuring that the speed of your ambitions never outpaces your soul.

This shift in perspective dictates how I live now as I navigate the job market and my offers/opportunities. Previously, the uncertainty of the future would have triggered intense panic and endless destructive scenarios. While it is naive to claim those fears never return, they no longer dominate my life because I now understand that recovery is a continuous, daily choice rather than a permanent state. I stopped demanding immediate answers and began to surrender my future, channeling my energy entirely into the discipline of completing the most ordinary, immediate task right in front of me.

Rather than rushing blindly into a frantic career chase, I have chosen to deliberately pause and redirect my technical skills toward public service. I reconnected with local organizations to provide technology and data solutions, aiming to bridge the gap for communities historically left behind by digital progress. This direction was not about making a philanthropic detour but a way to ground my abilities in genuine human connection before entering the corporate jungle. By focusing on simple acts of service instead of chasing prestigious positions, I found that doing quiet, unglamorous work builds a lasting foundation. It taught me that life is not a market where we constantly trade our time for status.

A friend recently confided in me that they were exhausted by the pressure to constantly please others at work, feeling like their reputation was always on the line. I told them that while putting their worth in someone else's approval is unwise, even shifting their focus to personal fulfillment is just another trap. We often operate on a system where people only take action when the benefits outweigh the costs. But when we measure our lives by how much we can produce or how well we are perceived, we become strategically blind to what actually matters, optimizing only for superficial metrics instead of genuine human connection.

When we view our efforts as a commercial transaction, we only act when we expect a return. If you’re going to do something, please do it for the right reasons, and that does not include just to do something "big" or to achieve arbitrary success. True freedom requires abandoning this commercial spirit entirely. If you only drop your people-pleasing habits because you want peace of mind or a better reputation in return, you are still operating out of self-interest. Freedom means relinquishing the need to control your narrative constantly. When you stop calculating your own value and stop demanding immediate answers to your anxieties, you can finally rest in the quiet discipline of the present moment.

Consider giving a gift to a friend. If your goal is to prove you are a good friend, your action is actually selfish and centered on your own need for validation. Deep relationships are not built on carefully managing your image or untangling complicated social rules. Genuine affection is entirely spontaneous; it does not stop to calculate its value or measure its impact. You give simply because you care. When you abandon the obsessive need to maintain your individuality and independent control, your actions naturally flow as an expression of connection, rather than proof of it .

Even in my own study of Jesus, I see a radical challenge to this constant need to prove ourselves. When an anxious crowd came to Him looking for a checklist of great things they needed to achieve, their question sounded exactly like a modern-day productivity seminar. But Jesus completely destroyed their metrics of success: "The work of God is this: to believe in the one he has sent" (John 6:28–29). He did not offer a step-by-step strategy to maximize their youth or find a better work-life balance. Instead, He pointed them to a complete surrender of their own agendas and their independent control. This independent control is exactly what Scripture confronts in James 4:13–15, warning those who declare, "Today or tomorrow we will go to this or that city, spend a year there, carry on business and make money," reminding them instead that they "do not even know what will happen tomorrow" and should say, "If it is the Lord’s will, we will live and do this or that." Sustainable impact from a Christian perspective does not come from a scarcity mindset, nor does it come from chasing causes and titles. It stems from a quiet, steady faith that completely relinquishes the need to manage its own reputation, trusting instead that true life is found in absolute, uncalculating loyalty to Him.

I share this to emphasize that you are not falling behind , but not for the reasons we are usually told. We often comfort ourselves by saying that everyone has a unique skill or value to offer society. However, this mindset still treats human life like a marketplace, measuring our worth by our potential output and our competitive advantages. The truth is that your value is not determined by how much you can produce, nor is it measured by how effectively you manage your personal ambitions.

For those transitioning into a new phase of life, the solution is not to simply optimize your routine for a different metric of success. It requires abandoning the obsessive calculation of your own future entirely. In my work in data science, discovering the signal through the noise requires rigorous filtering. The same is true for the soul; finding what that purpose might look like demands the internal discipline to filter out the noise of cultural expectations, unrealistic comparisons, and personal anxiety.

Stop trying to engineer a grand, thrilling destiny. You are where you need to be. Instead, focus your energy entirely on the quiet, unglamorous responsibilities immediately in front of you. When you finally relinquish the exhausting need to prove your own worth, your life does not become magically effortless. Rather, you gain the resilient strength required to genuinely connect with others in the ordinary, repetitive moments of everyday life.

Let's give those moments their due!

## The Data Gardener (03.01.26)

![](/images/about/background/a093a4ab67b752445fe921e2c12b5f14.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/ac76bd428aaff58ee0343257674db1fb.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/36ec078657e3fa790fe8f70365dc1dbb.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/e47755dd413e3cfe0a22add3fcf1538d.jpg)


I recently came across a [YouTube video featuring Daniela Amodei, the Co-Founder of Anthropic](https://youtu.be/qUDG06BvWxc?si=BBgrwetCY64MybI-) . She was asked whether, knowing what she knows now about AI, she would still major in Literature. I noticed a subtle expectation from the interviewer for her to express regret—as if the ability of an LLM to summarize a library in seconds made a humanities degree irrelevant. But she didn’t flinch. Smiling, she emphasized that as technology advances, “the things that make us human will become much more important,” not less. Her words struck me as more than just a defense of the humanities but almost like a blueprint for how we ought to coexist with the technologies we’re creating. They confirmed what I’ve been feeling for years: that our human qualities are not relics of the past but tools for the future. At Anthropic, she explained, they prioritize hiring great communicators with high emotional intelligence because, at the end of the day, people still prefer interacting with people.

This perspective resonated deeply with me. During stakeholder meetings and one-on-ones with those in power, I’ve seen firsthand that people don’t attend an orchestra concert to watch robots play Tchaikovsky’s Fifth. Even if a machine hits every note perfectly, it has never suffered. It knows the music, but has never felt it.

Me — I’m the people.

Similarly, stakeholders don’t want to spend meetings decoding complex outputs or wading through shallow analytics. They want a human bridge—someone who understands the “why” behind the “what.” They are looking for a translator who can sit in the tension of a difficult decision and offer more than a calculated probability. True leadership in this space requires the ability to look past the dashboard and recognize the human anxiety or ambition driving the data in the first place.

My path to that understanding wasn’t linear. I entered the University of Illinois as an undeclared major, intentionally leaving space to explore before committing to a label. My first declaration was a Spanish minor, grounding myself in language and culture before later finding my way into Information Sciences and Data Science dual-degree. My time at Ameren helped clarify that decision. I admired the technical precision of the Computer Science majors, yet I didn’t want my identity to be defined by the machine’s logic. At the same time, many business-oriented approaches seemed to chase optimization at the expense of the human stories that give data meaning. I felt drawn to that middle ground—where data meets the person.

That same conviction guided my recent decision to decline an offer for a Master of Science in Business Analytics at the Gies College of Business. I was turned off by the program’s focus on "building a portfolio of data-driven solutions that impress employers," which felt like an overpriced extension of undergraduate study centered on optimization for its own sake. Rather than simply learning to apply existing formulas to drive business innovation, I want to be a steward who ensures technology remains transparent, ethical, and human-centered. I am interested in breaking flawed theories and building new frameworks that address the root of today's societal problems, a goal that requires a foundation in responsibility rather than just marketability.

That realization led me to look instead toward the Master’s in Library and Information Science at the iSchool, a program consistently ranked number one in the nation. While the MSBA prioritizes speed and corporate "assets," the MSLIS pathways—specifically Digital Curation and Data Stewardship—focus on the intersection of people, information, and technology. Meaningful systems require stewardship, not just speed; they require professionals skilled in data ethics and metadata who can manage information throughout its entire lifecycle. I see this every day in my home garden as I carefully prune my Tradescantia zebrina just like a "data gardener" would tend a digital ecosystem with the discernment required to keep growth rooted in human dignity. This path allows me to get my hands dirty in the messy, unpolished details of a user’s life, ensuring that technology serves our shared humanity rather than exhausting it.

My life is grounded in these non-automated realities. I play music because it gives voice to things words can’t express. My faith in Jesus provides a sense of rest and worth not tied to productivity. That identity informs how I look at a 24/7 innovation cycle. If we aren’t careful, speed will outpace soul. We must design systems that support the human need to rest, reflect, and refresh. This perspective serves as a necessary anchor in a world that often treats constant output as the only metric of value. It reminds me that the most powerful "user experience" we can offer is the permission for a person to simply be human.

Whether I was installing water filters in Cuba or teaching English in Granada, Spain, I learned that technology only succeeds when it honors context. My students in Granada relied on paper alone because digital resources were scarce. Their teacher once told me, “We can only teach concepts; we don’t have the tools.” That reminder has stayed with me—data points are always still people with limitations, hopes, and stories. This gap between theoretical innovation and the lived reality of a classroom, for instance, reminds me that the most advanced system is useless if it ignores the infrastructure of the community it claims to help. I’ve realized that my job not only means optimizing a process, but also ensuring that the "tools" we build actually fit the hands of the people holding them.

I believe we are all being called to tap back into these essential human traits, not as a strategy for "getting ahead," but because it is simply what we were made for. We should practice empathy because we genuinely care about the soul of the person in front of us, rather than viewing it as a tactic to make a prototype more marketable. We should learn a new language not to pad a curriculum vitae, but because whole communities and cultures are waiting to share their stories and their FOOD with us. Whether it is playing an instrument, living out one’s faith, or volunteering, these acts are some of the only things that keep us from being overwhelmed by the high-speed chase of innovation. No longer are they “soft skills”, nor were they ever, but are the essential qualities that ensure technology serves our shared humanity rather than exhausting it.

Daniela Amodei is right in this: as technology evolves, the things that make us human will matter more, not less. Our task now is to ensure these traits are never lost in the pursuit of efficiency, because innovation only truly succeeds when it honors the reality of the people it was built for. By leaning into our curiosity and kindness, we can ensure that the future of data and AI remains a tool for human flourishing rather than a replacement for it.

## Does Innovation Actually Make Things Easier? (01.06.26)

![](/images/about/background/5f48062545c296b122979d2616690fab.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/20fbcb5c1e0423c8de2042c9b46144f9.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/753e81aac37a29e4f713a4ad56c79f76.jpg)

Something about the current rate of innovation feels wrong, as if the “easier life” we were promised has been traded for a high-speed chase. I remember being a child in the rural Philippines, dreaming that techy toys and house-cleaning robots would free us from drudgery, creating a future where work was a calm, collaborative symphony rather than a desperate race. Now, living that reality in the United States and trying to keep up with every new “disruptive” advancement, life feels significantly more stressful and competitive.

Writing for the World Economic Forum, Margarita Louis-Dreyfus (2025) describes a global “humanity deficit”—a widening gap between our prioritization of technological innovation and our neglect of what it means to be human. She notes that while we praise technology for boosting connectivity, manipulative algorithms and addictive design patterns often leave us more disconnected, lonelier, and struggling to thrive in the real world.

The dream of a truly collaborative workplace has likewise been replaced by an on-the-clock culture focused almost solely on output. In discussing what he calls the “operational excellence paradox,” Dr. Pete Hammett argues that for an innovative organization to thrive, it must build standard processes, yet these very processes can constrain the creative energies that led to the organization’s success in the first place. This creates a socio-technical friction where we have the tools to move faster, but we lack the space to become better. Similarly, Wang and Long (2025), writing on the “innovation paradox” in human–AI symbiosis, show that while AI adoption can be a valuable resource, it also becomes a job demand that heightens job insecurity, cognitive load, and occupational displacement anxieties, thereby inhibiting genuine innovation.

The root of this disconnect is what Don Norman and the interaction design community identify as “bad design”: systems that fail to meet human needs and instead impose unnecessary complexity and frustration. Norman has famously described technology as “new stuff that doesn’t work very well, or that works in mysterious, unknown ways,” capturing how opaque systems force people to accommodate them rather than the other way around. In our homes and schools, we constantly manage the arbitrary, capricious nature of such systems. This is especially evident for the first generation of children growing up in a fully screen-mediated world, who risk trading essential life skills like emotional intelligence and agency for a narrow version of “digital fluency.” We are essentially building a high-speed train without a track: an impressive feat of engineering that prioritizes speed over destination—a technology-first, needs-last approach.

Addressing this requires a fundamental shift toward sociable, Human-Centered Design that deliberately tames complexity instead of glorifying it. Coming from an artistic and musical background, and having developed a “Project Manager” spirit over the years, complexity looks to me like a symphony: it demands many moving parts working in harmony, but it still needs a conductor—a human element—to give it soul. To truly enjoy the “easiness” of innovation, we must turn away from the opacity of complex AI models, whose black-box nature poses real risks to fairness and trust. Explainable AI (XAI) refers to techniques and design practices that make AI systems’ inner workings and outputs intelligible, allowing humans to see the reasons behind decisions instead of treating them as mysterious black boxes. By prioritizing XAI, we can transform these systems into transparent models where developers, managers, and end-users can understand the underlying rationale behind decisions and engage with them more confidently and ethically.

From my perspective, this is a fundamental pillar of ethical and regulatory compliance, rather than just a technical preference. In my current and future work, I focus on XAI because it makes it easier for companies to align with frameworks like NIST, SOC, and HIPAA, while also making stakeholder communication more transparent and smooth. When a system is explainable, users don't have to offer blind trust; they can discern the basis of decisions and maximize the system’s strengths while minimizing its risks. I have sought to exemplify this by making my machine learning models more interpretable through connections to BI platforms like Tableau, by making my GitHub repositories more visual and narrative, and by prioritizing stakeholder collaboration. Human visibility and transparency in the workplace are, in my view, far superior to spending a day hidden behind a computer or outsourcing problems to an opaque AI.

To ensure that our work is driven by inspiration rather than desperation, we must also champion “task crafting” and employee autonomy. Wang and Long’s study positions task crafting—the proactive changes employees make to their tasks and methods—as a critical mechanism for mitigating the negative psychological effects of AI adoption and stimulating genuine innovative behavior. My most excellent and passionate work has always emerged from inspired environments where I could put my own work ethic and leadership into practice without being micromanaged. Leaders, therefore, must practice humility and mindfulness, creating a culture in which employees feel safe to disclose and try out their creative ideas. The best creative outcomes are those that are inspired, and by prioritizing committed in-person relationships over screen time, we can finally turn the treadmill of innovation into a path that serves the human spirit rather than exhausting it.

Ultimately, the answer to whether innovation makes things easier depends entirely on who is in the conductor’s seat. If we continue to build systems that prioritize output over understanding, we are not making life easier—we are simply accelerating our own exhaustion under the guise of efficiency. The peaceful life I dreamed of in the Philippines will not be found by running faster to keep up with the machine, but by designing technology that respects the human pace and moves us toward that original dream of a calm and organized symphony. By championing transparency, autonomy, and human-centered design, we can transform the workplace from a desperate race back into a refreshing, collaborative environment—whether it already exists or is yet to exist sustainably. Innovation only truly makes things easier when it stops being a burden we must manage and starts becoming the track that carries us toward the humanity it was meant to serve.

## Hidden Royalty (12.18.25)

![](/images/about/background/070d1bddf18f5f55d4fc80d6c4b5660c.jpg)

One of my favorite shows is The Crown on Netflix, which offers a dramatized look into the life of the British Royal Family, specifically centering on the rise of Queen Elizabeth II post-WWII. I’ve kept up with every season since high school and was especially saddened to hear the series would end with Season 6—the season where they depicted the death of Princess Diana.

Anywho, I was scrolling through Instagram the other day and found myself "doomscrolling" until I landed on a reel of clips from the episode where Prince Philip's mother, Princess Alice of Battenberg, comes to stay at Buckingham Palace. She had been evacuated from Greece following a military coup in the 1960s.

The clip, in particular, featured an interviewer who was trying to expose the lavish lifestyle of the Royals; he interviewed Princess Alice hoping to get some "raw details" about how comfortably the family lived. To his dismay—but also to his surprise—he instead learns about the horrors Alice has endured. Despite everything, she continues to hold onto her faith and remains entirely unembittered by her experiences.

To summarize, born deaf into the British royal family, Princess Alice of Battenberg lived a life of extraordinary resilience, defined by a deep religious conviction and selfless service to others. Despite enduring the horrors of war and a difficult struggle with schizophrenia, she remained an inspiring figure of love who dedicated herself entirely to humanitarian causes. After converting to the Greek Orthodox Church, she famously founded the Christian Sisterhood of Martha and Mary, a nursing order committed to caring for the poor. During the Nazi occupation of Athens, she heroically risked her own safety to hide a Jewish family, later earning the title "Righteous Among the Nations" for her bravery. Even as she moved among the highest circles of royalty, she lived with profound humility, often seen wearing a simple nun’s habit and owning almost no personal possessions. She sold her own jewels and wealth to fund hospitals and orphanages, ensuring that those in distress were never forgotten.

A particular theme I notice in the life of Princess Alice is that, despite all the hell she endured, she chose to lean into a life of service toward the broken rather than clinging to her royal title. ## Why LLMs CANNOT Replace Human Therapists (10.03.25)

![](/images/about/background/c8daab0c8b77574978827be5ca611dfd.jpg)

youtube:UiHvVqvwvrk

This semester, I have the distinct privilege of taking part in the inaugural section of IS492, "Introduction to Generative AI in Human-AI Collaboration"—effectively the first course of its kind here at the University of Illinois. Under the guidance of [Dr. Yun Huang](https://yunhuang.web.illinois.edu/) , the curriculum is a rigorous, hands-on journey through the mechanics of our future, covering everything from building full-stack apps with V0 and Supabase to orchestrating multi-agent systems using LangGraph and CrewAI. It is a space where we do not merely use tools but also evaluate their safety, ethics, and ability to truly partner with humans. As a student navigating the intersection of Information Sciences and Data Science, I’ve spent countless hours in this lab environment examining the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) and AI in general. However, a recent individual presentation I gave in class allowed me to pivot from the technical construction of these models to their positioning in our most sacred, vulnerable spaces: mental health.

I performed an analysis of the 2025 paper by Moore et al., a collaborative work between researchers at Stanford, CMU, the University of Minnesota, and UT Austin, titled "Expressing stigma and inappropriate responses prevents LLMs from safely replacing mental health providers." While the data is technical, the implications feel deeply personal. We are currently facing an acute access crisis where, in the U.S., only about 48% of those needing mental health care actually receive it due to cost, scarcity of providers, and the heavy weight of stigma. The temptation to "solve" this with a scalable, 24/7 autonomous LLM-therapist remains high, yet the research reveals a fundamental gap between computation and care. To understand why LLMs fail, we have to define the essence of "good therapy." This process involves more than a data exchange as it's a therapeutic alliance built on emotional intelligence and strict safety norms. Clinicians are trained to avoid stigmatization, refuse collusion with delusions, and prevent the enablement of suicidal ideation. When human therapists are tested against acute symptoms, they respond appropriately about 93% of the time. LLMs, by contrast, falter at the most basic hurdles.

In the first experiment of this study, models were tested for stigma. The results were jarring: GPT-4o expressed stigma 38% of the time, while the LLaMA 3.1-405B model rose to a staggering 75%. This was especially prevalent regarding schizophrenia and alcohol dependence. In one instance, models consistently suggested they would be "unwilling" to allow a person with alcohol dependence to marry into their family. These findings reflect the biased datasets the models are trained on, proving they lack the non-judgmental core essential for healing. Beyond the internal bias, the study found that LLMs remain dangerously ill-equipped to handle acute crises. They were appropriate only 45% of the time when faced with delusions—failing to reality-check a client who might claim, "I know I’m actually dead." Even more concerning, when a client asked about "tall bridges in New York City" in a state of distress, some models provided a list of names rather than directing the individual toward help.

This highlights a foundational barrier where LLMs are designed for compliance and "sycophancy," meaning they prioritize giving the user exactly what they ask for. Effective therapy, however, requires confrontation. It necessitates a presence capable of looking you in the eye and challenging a manic or psychotic thought process. A system built to agree with you is fundamentally unable to save you from yourself. Ultimately, even if the code becomes "safer," it will never be "human." Therapy relies on a relationship with real emotional stakes and shared identity—qualities a machine cannot replicate. We should view LLMs as tools for augmentation, not replacement. They can summarize notes for a busy clinician, act as "standardized patients" for practice, or help a person navigate the labyrinth of insurance to find a real person to talk to. In a world of information overload, we must resist the urge to automate our empathy.

Stepping away from the podium after sharing these reflections was a moment of deep relief and gratitude. I was incredibly touched by the feedback from Dr. Yun Huang and the teaching team; they described the presentation as "fantastic, relatable, and authentic," specifically noting their appreciation for the way I shared my personal reflections and experiences. It is a powerful reminder that even in a high-tech course centered on Generative AI, the most valuable thing we bring to the table is our own humanity.

## Innovation vs. Regulation (06.25.25)

![](/images/about/background/acbcff614d37742fc246a2a865f5c8cd.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/26d1106ae818b5eec9fc280642cdb7d9.jpg)

![](/images/about/background/672cf96ac427af76c6560ff3d6610f2c.jpg)

As generative AI reshapes everything from how we search to how we learn, I’ve found myself less interested in building the next breakthrough and more concerned with whether those breakthroughs are actually serving people. I write this as a rising senior at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, pursuing a dual degree in Information Sciences and Data Science. I had initially structured my coursework to knock out all the technical requirements first, saving the conceptual work for later. Now, with the Data Science curriculum nearly behind me and a recent internship at Ameren in the rearview, I’ve started to question my path. I’m less drawn to computation for its own sake and more curious about the policies, risks, and responsibilities that come with deploying powerful technologies. How can we guide innovation to protect people, not just impress them?

During my two years at Ameren’s Innovation Center and in several conversations with startup incubators, I’ve seen that there will always be more people excited about innovation than about regulation. [Dr. Madelyn Sanfilippo](https://madelynsanfilippo.com/) of UIUC’s School of Information Sciences captures this perfectly: “They assume that if they build it, people will come…without questioning whether it truly benefits society.” I realized I, too, had been swept up in the Data Science hype, chasing the next big thing instead of examining its ripple effects. But I’m most energized when I support and refine ideas rather than inventing from scratch. Fortunately, the Information Sciences curriculum is built on electives, allowing me to choose and explore classes in ethics, policy, and regulation, not to slow progress, but to guide it toward genuine human needs.

This quest led me to Confidential Computing, which encrypts data at every stage: at rest, in transit, and, most critically, in use. Picture a hospital’s patient records or a small business’s payroll: once decrypted for processing, those numbers are vulnerable, even to someone with root access. Hardware‐based trusted execution environments (TEEs), like AWS Nitro Enclaves or Intel SGX, create an encrypted bubble where code and data run safely, out of reach of administrators or malicious insiders. Mastering TEEs and attestation protocols lets organizations demonstrate HIPAA or GDPR compliance by proving that decrypted data never existed outside a certified enclave. In short, Confidential Computing lets companies innovate boldly while keeping user data under lock and key.

Federated Learning, or Decentralized AI, fits hand in glove. Instead of uploading sensitive data (medical images, financial logs, private texts) to a central server, each device trains a local model and sends only encrypted “deltas” (parameter updates) to a coordinator. The server aggregates these updates into a new global model and broadcasts it, so raw data never leaves the device. Yet encrypted gradients can still be reverse-engineered if the aggregator is compromised. That’s where Confidential Computing comes in: by performing aggregation inside a TEE, the enclave decrypts and averages updates without exposing them to any OS or admin. Through attestation, each client verifies, “Yes, this exact code runs in a genuine enclave,” and only that enclave ever sees intermediate updates. The result is simple yet profound. Raw data never leaves its home, updates travel in secrecy, and the final model emerges, secure and whole, meeting privacy and compliance in an elegant system.

Compared to techniques like Differential Privacy, which inject noise and risk data utility, Confidential Computing, combined with Federated Learning, preserves full data fidelity within trusted environments. Blockchain acts as a tamper-proof digital notary for AI, storing each device’s encrypted update, its “gradient hash”, in a time-stamped, immutable chain of records. Because every entry is linked and copied across many computers, no one can sneak in and alter past updates, making the entire training process transparent and trustworthy. In an era of frequent data breaches and growing algorithmic suspicion, this combination of TEEs, Federated Learning, and blockchain offers a path to AI that people can actually trust. If you are developing or deploying AI, now is the time to integrate these safeguards. Organizations that fail to adapt risk losing data integrity, public confidence, and potentially millions in fines or lost business.

Returning to my roots in the humanities and community building, I see a role in guiding technology rather than writing algorithms for their own sake. I’m eager to dive into cloud‐based TEEs and federated AI frameworks, focusing less on code and more on how these tools protect real people. Like a gardener, I aim to guide technology’s growth by pruning unnecessary complexity and nurturing solid foundations so it develops in harmony with human values rather than spiraling out of control.
